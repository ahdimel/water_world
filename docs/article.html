The Colorado River

The Price of Water

California is about to lose a tenth of its Colorado River water. We priced every way to replace it. We expected the answer to be desalination. It wasn't.

Co-authored by ahdimel and Claude
October 29, 2026 · All dollar figures in 2025 dollars unless noted

On October 22, 2026, Lake Mead fell to 6.78 million acre-feet, the least water it has held in a record that begins in 1937 and 23% of its 1942 peak. Five weeks earlier, Lake Powell upstream hit its own record low. An October storm that flooded towns in southeastern Utah lifted Powell about 10 feet. Hydrologists called it a drop in the bucket: the lake is still less than a quarter full.

In August, the federal government set new rules for sharing the river. The three lower-basin states must use 1.25 million acre-feet a year less in 2027 and 2028. Under the split they have proposed, California loses 440,000 acre-feet a year. In the deepest cuts the framework allows, its share could reach about 1.1 million. Nevada has sued to block it.

An acre-foot is about 326,000 gallons, roughly what two to three Southern California households use in a year. In the Imperial Valley, the river's largest single user, farmers pay around $20 an acre-foot for delivery. California cities pay an average of $512, 17 times what farm districts pay.

That gap is where this project began. We assumed that a river priced like this would eventually force the West to build supply: desalination plants on the coast, turning seawater into drinking water. So we built a cost model of every way Southern California could replace the water it is losing, with every input traced to a public source and every result given as a range. Desalination came in near the bottom of the list.

Priced by right

Western water is allocated mostly by legal right, under prices set when the rivers weren't under pressure. Since 2000, the index for household water, sewer and trash bills has risen 207 percent, against 92 percent for everything else. But that rise pays for pipes and treatment plants, not for scarcity. Farm water has stayed close to flat. Neither price tells anyone the river is running out.

Pay farmers not to use their water

The cheapest water in the West is the water farmers agree not to use. Federal and district programs pay them about $400 an acre-foot to leave it in the river. In the Palo Verde Valley, the region's wholesaler pays farmers to idle about 26,000 acres.

But water in the river is not water at the tap. Getting it more than 200 miles across the desert through the Colorado River Aqueduct and treating it adds about $680 an acre-foot. That is anywhere from $170, counting only the electricity to pump it, to $1,180 at the wholesaler's full rate. Delivered to a coastal city, farm water costs about $1,150 an acre-foot, including about $70 toward the Salton Sea, below. That's still the cheapest new water in Southern California. Carlsbad, the largest desalination plant in the Western Hemisphere, costs about 2.6 times as much.

The Salton Sea's bill

The Salton Sea, California's largest lake, survives mostly on Imperial Valley farm drainage. When those farmers sell water to cities, less reaches the lake, and its exposed lakebed blows dust over the roughly 650,000 people who live nearby. The 2003 deal that sends about 303,000 acre-feet a year to San Diego and the Coachella Valley capped the water agencies' liability at $133 million. The State of California is on the hook for the rest.

Controlling that dust adds about $115 to each Imperial acre-foot; Palo Verde water, which drains back into the river, adds nothing. But that assumes the state builds the dust control. If it doesn't, the cost of doing nothing, by the Pacific Institute's estimate, works out to about $4,500 per acre-foot, and paying farmers becomes one of the most expensive ways to get water.

The cheapest water in California is cheap because the public pays part of the bill.

Same plant, four times the price

Israel gets most of its drinking water from the sea. Its Sorek plant cost about $1,080 per cubic meter of daily capacity; Carlsbad cost $5,670. The same company, IDE Technologies, built Sorek and designed Carlsbad. Adjusted for Sorek's larger size, California builds desalination at about 4.1 times Israel's cost (between 3.9 and 4.6 times, depending on how strongly cost falls with size).

The usual suspects don't explain it. Both countries use the same kind of contract, both plants borrowed about 80 percent of their cost, and Carlsbad got the cheap version of the intake rules. What differs is time. Carlsbad's developer started in 1998 and got water flowing in 2015. Huntington Beach, a plant of the same size from the same developer, started the same year and was denied in 2022 after about $100 million of development work. Israel builds its plants in two to three years, on sites the government picks in advance.

Priced the way an investor would, as money held at risk for years and sometimes lost outright, California's permitting explains about 1.4 times of the gap. Roughly 3 times is left unexplained. The likeliest culprit isn't specific to desalination. Pure Water Southern California, the region's planned water-recycling plant, is budgeted at $75 million per million gallons a day of capacity, against $27 million for Carlsbad.

Two fixes are within California's reach. Public financing would cut about $390 an acre-foot, and permitting reform about $360 more. That still leaves a plant at about $2,290, well above the $1,790 it would cost at Israeli prices. Part of the remaining gap is electricity, which costs California industry about twice what it costs Israel's. Electricity alone is about $870 of every acre-foot.

What's on the table

So what can Southern California actually do to replace the river water it is losing? Here is every option we found, priced on the same footing: delivered and treated, financed with public debt, in 2025 dollars. The dot is our best estimate; the bar shows the plausible range.

Farm water, stormwater capture and tearing out lawns all cost roughly what cities pay for imported water today: $1,528 an acre-foot this year. Desalination starts at about $1,790 even at Israeli prices. The surprise is at the top: recycling wastewater into drinking water at Pure Water Southern California would cost $5,200 to $6,800 an acre-foot by the agency's own estimate, more than Carlsbad.

Price is only half of it. Each cheap option can supply only so much.

The verdict

$4,500
Hidden cost per Imperial acre-foot if the Salton Sea's dust is never controlled

The cheap water is real, but capped. What farmers can sell is about the size of California's cut. Whether it alone would cover the cut is a coin flip. Add stormwater and conservation and the cheaper options reach about 1.2 million acre-feet a year: comfortably more than today's cut, and probably enough even for the deepest cut the framework allows.

It depends on a promise. Farm water stays cheap only if California builds the dust control it took on at the Salton Sea in 2003. If it doesn't, paying farmers costs more than any reformed desalination plant.

When the expensive water comes, institutions set its price. If cuts outrun the cheap options, the next acre-foot comes from the sea. It would cost about $2,650 the way California builds today, or about $1,790 if it could build like Israel.

Caveats

The volumes are the model's weakest inputs. Farm sales rest on one program, in which California pledged 400,000 acre-feet a year for 2023 to 2026 (we allowed 250,000 to 700,000). The stormwater and conservation volumes are our estimates from statewide studies. The cost of moving water to the coast varies sixfold depending on how the aqueduct is counted. The model doesn't price fallowing's toll on farm towns, uses general inflation to adjust construction costs, and can't answer the political question of who should pay.

Price water properly, and the first things a market buys are fallow fields and torn-out lawns, not desalination plants.

Co-authored by ahdimel and Claude. Ahdimel framed the problem space, the current challenges, a hypothesis and the goals of this study, then advised on scope and method. Claude Opus, an advanced AI model, did most of the literature research, built a Python model, derived the numbers in this article and drafted the text.

Methods. Costs are levelized over 30 years and restated in 2025 dollars with the consumer price index. Uncertain inputs are drawn from their published ranges, 10,000 times. The rule deciding the article's conclusion was written into the code before the final run: farm water had to beat the cheapest plausible desalination in 90 percent of runs, and a second test compared its volume with California's cut.
