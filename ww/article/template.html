The Colorado River · An investigation

The Price of Water

We set out to show that the American West should be building desalination plants. Months of modeling later, the numbers had other ideas — about farms, a dying lake, and why California pays four times what Israel does for the same plant.

On $mead_low_date, Lake Mead fell to $mead_low — the least water the reservoir has held in a record that begins in 1937, and $mead_low_pct of its 1942 peak. Five weeks earlier, on $powell_low_date, Lake Powell upstream hit its own low: $powell_low, the least since it first filled.

Then a storm stalled over the Four Corners. The San Juan River flooded towns in southeastern Utah, and Powell rose about 10 feet in October. Hydrologists called it a drop in the bucket. As of this week Powell holds $powell_last million acre-feet. It is still less than a quarter full.

Two months ago the federal government finished rewriting the rules for sharing the river. Under the framework signed on Aug. 21, the three lower-basin states must use 1.25 million acre-feet a year less in 2027 and 2028. If they divide the cut as they have proposed, California's share is $cut acre-feet a year. The framework allows Lower Basin cuts of up to 3 million acre-feet in extreme conditions; if California bore the same share of that, it would lose about $cut_hi acre-feet a year. Nevada has sued to block the framework.

An acre-foot is the volume that would cover an acre of land a foot deep — about 326,000 gallons, or roughly what two to three Southern California households use in a year. It is the unit everything in this story is priced in.

This project began with a contradiction that anyone who reads about the Colorado River eventually runs into. The reservoirs are at record lows, and in the desert along the lower river, farms grow alfalfa — some of it shipped overseas — on water that costs them almost nothing. In the Imperial Valley, the largest single user of the river, farmers pay around $iid_rate an acre-foot for delivery. The average California city utility pays about $city_rate, $city_ag_ratio times what the average farm district pays.

Our starting hunch was that a river priced like that would mismanage itself into collapse, and that the fix was to build supply: desalination plants on the coast, running on cheap midday solar, storing water instead of electricity. We were wrong about that, and about several other things along the way. This is an account of what the evidence said instead.

How we did this

We built a cost model of every way Southern California could replace the water it is about to lose, from paying farmers to tearing out lawns to building plants that turn seawater into drinking water. Every number comes from a cited public source or from arithmetic that our tests re-check. The model runs each calculation $n_draws times across the plausible range of every input, so results come as ranges, not single numbers.

Before the final run, we wrote down the rule that would decide what this article argued, and we did not change it after seeing the results. Where the evidence went against what we expected, we say so — there is a list near the end.

Priced by right, not by scarcity

Water in the American West is mostly allocated by legal right, and the prices attached to those rights were set when the rivers were not under pressure. Physical scarcity has changed a great deal since. The price signal has not.

Our first claim about prices was wrong. We assumed household water had stayed cheap. It hasn't: since January 2000, the price index for water, sewer and trash service has risen $cpi_water percent, against $cpi_all percent for prices overall — a real increase of about $cpi_real percent. But that increase pays for pipes, pumps and treatment plants. It is not a scarcity signal. Farm water, meanwhile, has stayed close to flat in nominal terms, which means it has gotten cheaper in real terms. Neither price tells anyone that the river is running out.

The 12-to-1 that wasn't

The desalination idea died in the first week. Seawater plants are expensive mostly because they are expensive to build, so idling one to chase cheap electricity strands the capital. Published studies of flexible operation found little or no saving under today's power tariffs.

Worse for the idea, there was a much cheaper option. In the Palo Verde Valley, on the California side of the river, the region's main water wholesaler pays farmers to leave about $pvid_acres acres unplanted and the water in the river. Worked out per acre-foot, that came to about $$280. Carlsbad, the largest desalination plant in the Western Hemisphere, sells water to San Diego for about $press_price. Paying farmers looked roughly $phase0_ratio times cheaper.

That comparison has a flaw, and it took us until the final phase to see it. The $$280 is water left in the river more than 200 miles from the coast. Carlsbad's price is water delivered, treated, into San Diego's pipes. To compare them, river water has to be pumped across the desert through the Colorado River Aqueduct and treated — and that turns out to be the largest part of its cost.

Federal and district programs now pay farmers roughly $ag_payment an acre-foot to conserve. Moving that water to the coast and treating it adds about $ag_conveyance. The range is wide, from $conv_lo if you count only the electricity to pump it to $conv_hi if you count the full rate the region's wholesaler charges. Add a share of the Salton Sea's dust bill, which comes next, and paying farmers delivers water to a coastal city for about $ag_total an acre-foot.

That is still the cheapest new water in Southern California. But Carlsbad's water costs about $delivered_ratio times as much, not $phase0_ratio.

The lake that sends the bill

The Salton Sea is California's largest lake. It formed when the Colorado River broke through an irrigation canal in 1905, and it has survived since then mostly on the Imperial Valley's farm drainage. When farmers there use less water, less reaches the lake.

In 2003, a set of agreements known as the Quantification Settlement Agreement began moving about $qsa_volume acre-feet a year of conserved Imperial Valley water to San Diego and the Coachella Valley. The deal capped the water agencies' liability for the lake at $qsa_cap. Beyond that, the State of California is the backstop. As the lake shrinks it exposes dry lakebed, and wind lifts the dust into the lungs of the roughly 650,000 people who live nearby. A 2025 study found the dust worsened after 2011, as the transfer ramped up, and that the increase fell hardest on disadvantaged communities. After a single year of paid conservation in 2023, the lake lost about $playa_2023 acres of surface.

The cheapest water in California is cheap because someone else is paying part of the bill.

This is the same pattern as the farm prices: the cost sits with the public rather than the buyer. Our first estimate put that hidden cost at $salton_full per acre-foot transferred. That figure blamed the transfer for all of the lake's decline and counted every dollar of restoration as dust control. Neither is true.

Inflow to the lake is projected to fall from about 1.25 million acre-feet a year to about 750,000. The transfer accounts for about $qsa_share of that decline, so we charged it $qsa_share of the bill. The state's projects mix habitat and dust suppression, and we assumed $dust_share of the spending is for dust. That leaves $salton_dust per Imperial acre-foot. The cost applies only to water from the Imperial Valley. Palo Verde drains back into the river, and our own first estimate had charged it to the wrong valley.

All of that assumes the state actually builds the dust control it is legally responsible for. If it doesn't, the right price is the damage instead. The Pacific Institute put the cost of doing nothing at the Salton Sea at $$29 billion to $$70 billion over 30 years. Attributed the same way, that comes to about $salton_damage per acre-foot, roughly $salton_ratio times the cost of mitigation. At that price, paying farmers stops being the cheapest water in the region and becomes one of the most expensive.

Same engineers, a quarter of the price

Israel gets most of its drinking water from the sea. Sorek, south of Tel Aviv, is one of the largest seawater plants in the world. It cost about $sk_unit per cubic meter of daily capacity, in today's dollars. Carlsbad cost $cb_unit, about $raw_gap times as much. The company that built Sorek, IDE Technologies, also designed Carlsbad and operates it. So whatever explains the difference, it is not engineering know-how.

Some of the gap is scale: Sorek is more than three times bigger, and big plants are cheaper per unit. Adjusted for size, California still builds desalination capacity at about $gap times Israel's cost (between $gap_lo and $gap_hi times, depending on how strongly cost scales with size). Our first estimate said five. Part of that was an accounting artifact: Carlsbad's cost includes a 10-mile pipeline, and Sorek's does not.

We tested the obvious explanations and most of them failed. Contract structure: both countries use take-or-pay contracts, in which the buyer pays for a minimum volume whether it takes it or not. Borrowing: both plants were about 80 percent debt-financed. Environmental rules on intakes: Carlsbad got the cheap version, an open intake and an existing power-plant outfall.

What does differ is time. Carlsbad's developer started work in 1998 and reached permits and financing in December 2012; water flowed three years later. Huntington Beach — same developer, same size, same start year — was denied unanimously in May 2022 after about $hb_spend of development spending. Israel builds its plants in two to three years on sites the government picks in advance.

We priced that delay the way an investor would. A developer's money sits at risk for years and can be lost outright, so the projects that do get built have to earn it back. Done that way, California's permitting process explains about $permit_x times of the $gap-times gap. Roughly $residual_x times survives full permitting reform, and we cannot name what it is.

The best candidate is the most uncomfortable one. Studies of American infrastructure find that the U.S. pays far more than peer countries for the same project types, mostly because of administrative overhead, procurement practice and one-off design. The evidence here points the same way: Pure Water Southern California, the region's flagship water-recycling project, is budgeted at $pwsc_mgd per million gallons a day of capacity, against $cb_mgd for Carlsbad. Whatever is wrong, it is not specific to desalination.

Two changes are within California's control. Financing the plant with public debt, as the region's wholesaler plans to do for its recycling project, would cut about $public_saving an acre-foot. Reforming permitting would cut about $reform_saving more. Even then, the plant would cost about $ladder2, not the $ladder3 it would cost at Israeli construction prices. Part of the remaining difference is electricity, which costs California industry about twice what it costs Israel's. Energy alone is about $energy_af of every acre-foot of Californian desalinated water.

The $$3,500 question

Midway through, we decided the widely quoted price of Carlsbad water, $press_price an acre-foot, was wrong. The Water Authority's own figure, as cited in a 2025 UC Davis law journal article, was $$2,302 to $$2,559, and our model agreed with it almost exactly.

We were comparing dollars from different years. The 2012 contract set a price of $contract_2012 at the minimum purchase volume. In today's dollars, that is $contract_2025. The current figure is that contract a decade of inflation later; the remaining 9 percent or so is consistent with later work on the plant. Once every number was put in 2025 dollars, the model matched four published prices from three different years, each within 5 percent. Our earlier, closer match had been partly two errors canceling out.

The most expensive option is the one often presented as the cheaper alternative to desalination. Price is only half the question. Each cheap option can supply only so much water.

What the model decided

Before the last run, we wrote down three possible conclusions and the rule for choosing among them. If paying farmers beat even the cheapest plausible desalination in at least 90 percent of runs, farm water would be the region's cheapest water. A second test would then decide whether its volume was large enough to cover California's cut.

Paying farmers beat desalination at Israeli prices in $p_cheaper percent of runs. Whether farm conservation alone could cover the $cut-acre-foot cut was a coin flip, $p_capacity percent. Our best guess of how much farmers can sell is about the size of the cut itself. At the framework's worst case it can't come close.

The cheap water is real, and it is capped. Paying farmers is the cheapest water in the region, and the ceiling on it is the story. Today's cut is about as large as what farmers can sell. Add stormwater and conservation, which also beat any desalination plant, and the cheaper options together come to about $cheap_median acre-feet a year. That covers California's current cut in $p_cheap_cut of runs, and the worst case in $p_cheap_hi.

The cheap water depends on a promise. Farm water is cheap only if California builds the dust control at the Salton Sea that it took on in 2003. If it doesn't, the hidden bill is roughly $salton_ratio times larger, and paying farmers becomes more expensive than any reformed desalination plant.

When the expensive water comes, California will pay for its institutions. If cuts deepen beyond what the cheaper options can cover, the next acre-foot comes from the sea. It will cost about $ladder1 under the current way of doing things, or about $ladder3 if California could build like Israel. Only part of that difference is something we can name.

We started this project believing markets price scarcity better than the West's current system. Nothing here contradicts that. But the market answer turned out not to be the one we expected: price water properly and the first things you buy are farmland left fallow and lawns torn out, not desalination plants.

What we got wrong along the way

  1. Desalination running on cheap solar. The idea that started the project. Plants are too capital-heavy to idle.
  2. "Water hasn't kept pace with incomes." Household water bills rose about $cpi_real percent faster than inflation. The real gap is between farm and city prices.
  3. Desalination costs 12 times as much as farm water. That compared water in the river with water at the tap. Delivered, the ratio is about $delivered_ratio.
  4. The Salton Sea's cost was charged to the wrong valley, and blamed the transfer for all of the lake's decline. Attributed properly, it fell by more than half.
  5. We retired the $$3,500 Carlsbad price, then reinstated it. Our earlier figures were in older dollars.
  6. "California builds desalination at five times Israel's cost." It is about $gap times, once Carlsbad's pipeline is taken out.
  7. "Permitting explains the gap." It explains about $permit_x times of it.

What we don't know

The volumes are the weakest part of the model. How much water farmers will sell is anchored on a single program, in which California pledged 400,000 acre-feet a year for 2023 to 2026; we allowed 250,000 to 700,000. The stormwater and conservation volumes are our own estimates from statewide studies. The cost of moving river water to the coast spans a factor of six depending on how the aqueduct's costs are counted. The dust share of the state's Salton Sea spending is an assumption.

The model also leaves things out. The cost of fallowing to farm towns isn't priced: Palo Verde already has nearly a third of its farmland enrolled. Dollar figures are adjusted with general inflation, which understates how fast construction costs have risen since 2020. We did not model plants that run harder when power is cheap, or desalination on ships. And cost is only one way to decide. Who owns the water, and who pays, are political questions this model can't answer.

Methods and sources

The model is written in Python and tested: automated tests re-derive every figure we computed ourselves, and pin each result that went against our expectations so it can't quietly drift. Costs are levelized over 30 years. Every option is priced in 2025 dollars, restated with the consumer price index (October 2025 is missing because of the federal shutdown, so 2025 is averaged over 11 months). Seawater plants are modeled from their construction cost, financing, energy use and operating costs. The model was checked against published prices it was not tuned to:

Uncertain inputs are sampled from triangular distributions over their published or allowed ranges, $n_draws times. The decision rule was written into the code before the first full run. The thresholds themselves did not change.
