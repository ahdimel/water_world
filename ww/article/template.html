The Colorado River

The Price of Water

California is about to lose a tenth of its Colorado River water. We priced every way to replace it. We expected the answer to be desalination. It wasn't.

On $mead_low_date, Lake Mead fell to $mead_low, the least water it has held in a record that begins in 1937 and $mead_low_pct of its 1942 peak. Five weeks earlier, Lake Powell upstream hit its own record low. An October storm that flooded towns in southeastern Utah lifted Powell about 10 feet. Hydrologists called it a drop in the bucket: the lake is still less than a quarter full.

In August, the federal government set new rules for sharing the river. The three lower-basin states must use 1.25 million acre-feet a year less in 2027 and 2028. Under the split they have proposed, California loses $cut acre-feet a year. In the deepest cuts the framework allows, its share could reach about $cut_hi. Nevada has sued to block it.

An acre-foot is about 326,000 gallons, roughly what two to three Southern California households use in a year. In the Imperial Valley, the river's largest single user, farmers pay around $iid_rate an acre-foot for delivery. California cities pay an average of $city_rate, $city_ag_ratio times what farm districts pay.

That gap is where this project began. We assumed that a river priced like this would eventually force the West to build supply: desalination plants on the coast, turning seawater into drinking water. So we built a cost model of every way Southern California could replace the water it is losing, with every input traced to a public source and every result run $n_draws times across the plausible range of its inputs. Desalination came in near the bottom of the list.

Priced by right

Western water is allocated mostly by legal right, under prices set when the rivers weren't under pressure. Since 2000, the index for household water, sewer and trash bills has risen $cpi_water percent, against $cpi_all percent for everything else. But that rise pays for pipes and treatment plants, not for scarcity. Farm water has stayed close to flat. Neither price tells anyone the river is running out.

Farm water, delivered

The cheapest water in the West is the water farmers agree not to use. Federal and district programs pay them about $ag_payment an acre-foot to leave it in the river. In the Palo Verde Valley, the region's wholesaler pays farmers to idle about $pvid_acres acres.

But water in the river is not water at the tap. Getting it more than 200 miles across the desert through the Colorado River Aqueduct and treating it adds about $ag_conveyance an acre-foot. That is anywhere from $conv_lo, counting only the electricity to pump it, to $conv_hi at the wholesaler's full rate. Delivered to a coastal city, farm water costs about $ag_total an acre-foot. That's still the cheapest new water in Southern California. Carlsbad, the largest desalination plant in the Western Hemisphere, costs about $delivered_ratio times as much.

The Salton Sea's bill

That price includes a cost the buyer doesn't see. The Salton Sea, California's largest lake, has survived for a century mostly on Imperial Valley farm drainage. When those farmers sell water to cities, less reaches the lake, and its exposed lakebed blows toxic dust over the roughly 650,000 people who live nearby. The 2003 deal that moves about $qsa_volume acre-feet a year from Imperial to San Diego and the Coachella Valley capped the water agencies' liability at $qsa_cap. The State of California is on the hook for the rest.

The transfer accounts for about $qsa_share of the lake's shrinking inflow. Charging it that share of the state's dust-control spending comes to about $salton_dust per Imperial acre-foot. Water from Palo Verde, which drains back into the river, carries none.

The cheapest water in California is cheap because the public pays part of the bill.

That estimate assumes the state actually builds the dust control. If it doesn't, the right price is the damage instead. The Pacific Institute put the cost of doing nothing at the Salton Sea at $$29 billion to $$70 billion over 30 years. Attributed the same way, that is about $salton_damage per acre-foot, and at that price paying farmers becomes one of the most expensive ways to get water.

Same plant, four times the price

Israel gets most of its drinking water from the sea. Its Sorek plant cost about $sk_unit per cubic meter of daily capacity; Carlsbad cost $cb_unit. The same company, IDE Technologies, built Sorek and designed Carlsbad. Adjusted for Sorek's larger size, California builds desalination at about $gap times Israel's cost (between $gap_lo and $gap_hi times, depending on how strongly cost falls with size).

The usual suspects don't explain it. Both countries use the same kind of contract, both plants borrowed about 80 percent of their cost, and Carlsbad got the cheap version of the intake rules. What differs is time. Carlsbad's developer started in 1998 and got water flowing in 2015. Huntington Beach, a plant of the same size from the same developer, started the same year and was denied in 2022 after about $hb_spend of development work. Israel builds its plants in two to three years, on sites the government picks in advance.

Priced the way an investor would, as money held at risk for years and sometimes lost outright, California's permitting explains about $permit_x times of the gap. Roughly $residual_x times is left unexplained. The likeliest culprit isn't specific to desalination. Pure Water Southern California, the region's planned water-recycling plant, is budgeted at $pwsc_mgd per million gallons a day of capacity, against $cb_mgd for Carlsbad.

Two fixes are within California's reach. Public financing would cut about $public_saving an acre-foot, and permitting reform about $reform_saving more. That still leaves a plant at about $ladder2, well above the $ladder3 it would cost at Israeli prices. Part of the remaining gap is electricity, which costs California industry about twice what it costs Israel's. Electricity alone is about $energy_af of every acre-foot.

The cheap water is real, but capped. What farmers can sell is about the size of California's cut. Whether it alone would cover the cut is a coin flip. Add stormwater and conservation and the cheaper options reach about $cheap_median acre-feet a year. That covers the cut in $p_cheap_cut of runs and the deepest cut the framework allows in $p_cheap_hi.

It depends on a promise. Farm water stays cheap only if California builds the dust control it took on at the Salton Sea in 2003. If it doesn't, paying farmers costs more than any reformed desalination plant.

When the expensive water comes, institutions set its price. If cuts outrun the cheap options, the next acre-foot comes from the sea. It would cost about $ladder1 the way California builds today, or about $ladder3 if it could build like Israel.

Price water properly, in other words, and the first things a market buys are fallow fields and torn-out lawns, not desalination plants.

Caveats

The volumes are the model's weakest inputs. Farm sales rest on one program, in which California pledged 400,000 acre-feet a year for 2023 to 2026 (we allowed 250,000 to 700,000). The stormwater and conservation volumes are our estimates from statewide studies. The cost of moving water to the coast varies sixfold depending on how the aqueduct is counted. The model doesn't price fallowing's toll on farm towns, uses general inflation to adjust construction costs, and can't answer the political question of who should pay.
